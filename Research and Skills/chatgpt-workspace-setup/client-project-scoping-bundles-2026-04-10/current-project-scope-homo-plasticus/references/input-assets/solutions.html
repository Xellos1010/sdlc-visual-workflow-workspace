From the Book

The Quick Action Card — 3 Core Rules

These three rules, drawn directly from the book's Quick Action Card, cover the majority of daily plastic exposure for most people. Master these first.

🔥

Don't Heat It

Never heat food or drink in plastic. Heat dramatically accelerates chemical and particle leaching.

📦

Don't Store In It

Use glass, stainless, or ceramic for food and drink storage. Plastic leaches even at room temperature over time.

🚫

Don't Drink From It

Eliminate plastic bottles. Filter tap water. Bottled water contains up to 22× more microplastics than filtered tap.

Start Here

The 12-Step Quick Action Card

The complete action guide from the book — 12 evidence-based steps organized by priority. These are not lifestyle preferences. They are exposure-modifying interventions with measurable biological relevance.

Start with steps 1–3. Each step you take reduces your daily plastic burden. You do not need to do all 12 at once.

1
Filter your drinking water. Solid-carbon block or reverse osmosis filter. Bottled water is not the solution — it is part of the problem.
2
Stop heating food in plastic. Transfer to glass or ceramic before microwaving. This single step eliminates a massive daily exposure source.
3
Switch to glass and stainless food storage. Eliminate plastic containers, especially for fatty or acidic foods, which accelerate leaching.
4
Replace non-stick cookware with cast iron, stainless, or ceramic. PTFE and PFAS coatings degrade with heat, releasing particles into food.
5
Add a laundry microfiber filter. Washing synthetic textiles is one of the largest microfiber release events. A filter captures them before they enter waterways.
6
Improve indoor air quality. HEPA air purifier and vacuum. Microplastic fibers accumulate in household dust — especially on floors where children spend time.
7
Choose natural fiber clothing. Cotton, wool, linen, and hemp shed far fewer particles than polyester, nylon, or acrylic. Prioritize natural fibers for items worn close to the skin.
8
Reduce processed and plastic-packaged food. Fresh, whole foods in minimal or non-plastic packaging represent a significant reduction in dietary microplastic intake.
9
Switch personal care products to natural formulations. Avoid synthetic polymer ingredients in cosmetics, sunscreens, and body care. Check labels for polyethylene, polypropylene, or acrylates.
10
For infants: use glass bottles, not polypropylene. PP baby bottles heated during formula preparation release millions of microplastic particles per liter. Glass is the safe alternative.
11
During pregnancy: minimize all plastic contact. Microplastics cross the placental barrier. The fetal window of hormonal development (weeks 8–24) has no do-over. Precautionary reduction is the rational choice.
12
Reduce single-use plastics systematically. Reusable bags, metal straws, stainless travel mugs, and loose-leaf tea (not plastic-sealed bags) — each swap compounds into meaningful reduction over time.
